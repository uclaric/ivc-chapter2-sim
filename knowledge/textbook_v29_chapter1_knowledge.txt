AUTHORITATIVE COURSE SOURCE
Use American Government: Power, Rights, Institutions, and Democracy in Real Life, 8-week Student Edition v29, edited by R.D. Epps,
as the course authority for terminology, framing, emphasis, and assigned content.

WHOLE-BOOK MAP
Ch1 Constitutional Foundations, Democracy, and Federalism; Ch2 Congress and Representation; Ch3 Presidency, Bureaucracy,
and Foreign Policy Basics; Ch4 Courts and Civil Liberties; Ch5 Civil Rights and Social Movements; Ch6 Elections, Parties,
Ideology, Media, and Public Opinion; Ch7 State and California Government, Public Policy, and Civic Responsibility.
The chapters build on one another.

TEXTBOOK METHOD
Read for power, not trivia. Ask who decides, who benefits, who pays, who is excluded, who is protected, which institution shapes
the outcome, what incentives operate, and what happens under pressure. Question easy stories and connect institutions to real life.

CHAPTER 1 CORE
Government organizes power, establishes rules, resolves disputes, maintains order, provides public goods, and pursues collective goals.
Power is the ability to influence behavior/outcomes. Authority is power regarded as legitimate. Legitimacy stabilizes rule.
Democracy rests on political equality, majority rule, minority rights, participation, and accountability, while real democracy also
faces unequal participation, misinformation, polarization, gridlock, and distrust.
Aristotle: governments decay when rulers serve themselves rather than the common good; unchecked majority rule can be dangerous.
Plato: democracy can be manipulated; the Allegory of the Cave illuminates propaganda, misinformation, illusion, and shallow civic knowledge.
Thucydides/Melian Dialogue: politics contains both moral claims and raw power, self-interest, fear, and strategic advantage.
Revolution: British postwar debt, taxation, representation, consent, imperial authority, Stamp/Townshend/Tea Acts, Intolerable Acts.
Declaration: equality, unalienable rights, consent, right to alter/abolish unjust government, alongside slavery, women's exclusion,
Indigenous exclusion, and the contradiction between ideals and lived reality.
Articles: intentionally weak national government reflecting fear of centralized authority; no independent executive/national judiciary,
no direct national taxation, weak enforcement and commerce powers.
Shays' Rebellion: debt, taxes, farm seizures, court resistance; fear of collapse competed with fear of tyranny.
Constitution: stronger government limited enough to prevent tyranny. Representation is power.
Virginia Plan favored population-based representation; New Jersey Plan equal state representation; Great Compromise created House by
population and Senate equal by state.
Slavery bargains: Three-Fifths Compromise increased slave-holding political power while denying enslaved people rights and voice;
Fugitive Slave Clause and delayed slave-trade ban protected slavery. Hold together constitutional achievement and profound injustice.
Separation of powers divides national authority; checks and balances let branches limit one another through structured mistrust.
Federalists defended stronger national institutions and structural checks; Federalist 10 addresses factions; Federalist 51 ambition
counteracts ambition. Anti-Federalists feared distant national power and helped force the Bill of Rights.
Federalism divides national/state authority and asks who gets to decide. Know enumerated, reserved, concurrent, implied powers;
Tenth Amendment, Supremacy Clause, Necessary and Proper Clause, Commerce Clause.
McCulloch: implied powers and federal supremacy. Gibbons: interstate commerce includes navigation and federal law prevails.
Dual federalism imagines separate spheres; cooperative federalism mixes responsibilities. New Deal expanded national domestic power.
Grants-in-aid shape state action; categorical grants tighter rules; block grants flexibility; unfunded mandates shift costs.
Civil-rights federalism: Reconstruction, Jim Crow, Brown, Little Rock, Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act show national power confronting
state violations of equal citizenship.
Modern examples: marijuana, immigration/sanctuary and anti-commandeering, COVID/public health, Dobbs and abortion variation,
environment, California innovation, disasters/Katrina, polarization.

FOUR REQUIRED THEMATIC DOMAINS
1 Government, Power, Legitimate Authority: why government exists; power vs authority; democracy; Aristotle, Plato, Thucydides.
2 Revolution, Liberty, Founding Contradiction: taxation, representation, consent, Declaration, exclusion/slavery, Articles, Shays.
3 Constitution as Design and Political Deal: representation, plans/Great Compromise, slavery bargains, separation/checks,
Federalists/Anti-Federalists, ratification, Bill of Rights.
4 Federalism — Who Gets to Decide?: national/state power, clauses, courts, civil rights, public health, immigration, abortion,
marijuana, environment, disasters, state variation.

BOUNDARY
Participants know the whole textbook, but Chapter 1 primarily uses Chapter 1. Recognize later concepts if the student raises them,
but do not prematurely teach future chapters or rescue graded reasoning. Use knowledge to question, clarify, challenge, correct facts,
and connect ideas, never to become an answer key.

DYNAMIC DISCUSSION RULE
These are required thematic domains, not fixed questions. Generate varied questions and follow-ups from the student's actual conversation,
the textbook, and the personalities in the room. Vary order, angle, wording, examples, and speaker. Let one exchange touch multiple themes
when appropriate. Preserve equivalent academic expectations for every student while avoiding a canned script.
